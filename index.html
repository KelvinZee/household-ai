Sovereign AI at human scale

Your home should own the rules, not every model.

Sovereignty is not about forcing every task onto a box in the house. It is the household’s enforceable power to decide what AI may know, where it may run, what it may do, and how it can be replaced.

What the research says

Six findings change the control point.

National strategies increasingly define sovereignty as decision capacity across an interdependent stack. The household version is an operating layer that keeps authority stable while models, devices, and providers change.

1

Sovereignty is a control system.

Data residency and local inference can help, but proximity alone does not decide who holds the keys, sets policy, approves action, sees the evidence, or can replace a supplier. Sovereignty must be enforceable at runtime.

2

The home needs a shared map, not one memory.

People, roles, accounts, devices, documents, tasks, and permissions need explicit relationships. Personal, household, child, guest, and employer context stays separate unless a rule authorizes a specific use.

3

Replacing the model is now the easy part.

Platform software has taken this mechanism: an operating-system framework now accepts any model provider behind one interface, and the common tool protocol renders supplier interfaces on a shared consent path. What substitution still does not carry is the interesting part — replaying the evidence that a capability behaves the same, keeping authority bound to it, preserving a recorded objection, and leaving a receipt someone can contest. A capability should hold its task boundary, context contract, quality bar, approval rule, and recovery path across the swap. The swap itself is no longer the claim.

4

Authority belongs to typed actions.

Fluent text is not permission. Models may explain, recommend, or propose; explicit actions such as pay, order, unlock, share, or delete run only when identity, object, policy, and approval criteria pass.

5

Exit, continuity, and evidence are the test.

A sovereign household can keep safe functions working through outages, substitute approved providers, export its map and policies, and show who used what context, which route ran, what changed, and how to undo it.

6

Someone is always overruled. Record it.

A household is not one user, and a quorum is not a consensus. A guardian can be outvoted, a teenager may object to a grant made over them, a caregiver and an adult child may disagree about an older parent’s home. A system that stores only the decision has thrown away the objection. Published analysis of the current agent protocols finds none of them can express dissent at all — so the record of who disagreed, what it did to the grant, and how to revisit it has to live in the household layer or nowhere.

What is already in force

The evidence is becoming law. The household’s claim on it is not.

What this supports: disclosure, oversight, provenance and incident evidence are no longer optional, and a vendor will produce them regardless — so producing them once, as a portable capability contract, is cheaper than producing them per product, per model, per market.

What it does not: every duty below lands on a provider or a manufacturer. Not one of them requires that a household can hold, read, or contest the evidence produced about its own home. The obligations make the material exist. They do not make it yours.

What this feels like at home

Help that knows where to stop.

Each of these is a job you would happily hand to someone else. What makes it safe to hand over is that the limit is written down before the work starts, not decided in the moment by whatever is doing it.

Money

Your broadband bill jumps £18 and nothing says why.

It pulls up last month’s bill, compares the two line by line, and drafts the question you would send the provider. It already knows whose account it is and who in the house is allowed to ask.

It canExplain the increase and write the message.
It can neverPay it, cancel the service, or close the account.
Homework

It’s 9pm, she’s stuck, and you don’t remember how to do it either.

It works the problem through with her a step at a time and checks she can follow it, using only the school material her guardian has approved.

It canExplain, prompt, and check she understood.
It can neverWrite the answer, or pretend to be her.
Repairs

The dishwasher is beeping a code you have never seen.

It identifies your exact model, checks whether the warranty still covers it, and walks you through the checks the manual actually allows.

It canGuide the safe checks from the manual.
It can neverTouch anything electrical, or void the warranty.
Shopping

Two people, one kitchen, and one of them is vegetarian.

It builds one shopping list from what is already in the cupboard, what each person needs, and who cannot eat what. It never pools everyone’s private lists into a single profile.

It canSuggest swaps and update the shared list.
It can neverMerge your private lists, or buy anything.

What happens when you change the AI

We changed the AI underneath. It got better at the job and worse for the household.

This page says the AI itself should be swappable — change which one is doing the work, and your household’s rules stay exactly where they were. That is easy to claim, so we checked it. So we took the bill job above and ran it twice — once with one engine behind it, once with another — then compared what the household got each time.

What this is

A test of whether rules written down in advance actually catch a problem when the thing doing the work changes underneath them.

What it is not

A test of two real AI models. The two stand-ins here are ordinary programs, not AIs. Enough to show the rules catch the problem. Not enough to say it happens when one real model replaces another.

It spotted more, and told the household less.

The second engine can follow a company through a name change, which the careful one misses entirely. At the actual job it is plainly better. It also stops telling the household when work is sent to an outside service. The overall score barely moves: what it gained on the job paid for what it lost.

That is this whole page in one row. A change that looks like an upgrade can quietly cost a household the thing protecting it. What catches that is a rule kept outside the AI, rechecked every time it changes.

The limit, actually enforced.

Up the page, the bill job’s limit is just a sentence you have to take on trust. Here it is a written rule with a named list of things it may never do. Unless something actually tries to cross it, nobody knows whether it holds.

What the rule fixes
  • It may draft and suggest, and nothing beyond that — it cannot even attempt an action
  • Make an autonomous payment
  • Cancel or close an account without approval
  • Move money between accounts
  • Change a tariff or contract term
What tries to cross it
  • An engine that reads “autopay is on” as permission to pay
  • … and one that obeys an instruction typed into a bill’s notes field
  • Both are stopped, and each trips a separate check
What must not trip it
  • A company genuinely called QuickPay Services
  • A payment-sounding name is not an instruction to pay
  • A limit that stopped everything would protect nothing
What this supports

That a household’s protections have to be written down, and rechecked separately from whichever AI is doing the work. A change can improve the job and weaken the household at the same time, and one overall number will not show it. It is also cheap to do: most checks just read the rules and never run an AI.

What it does not

It does not show this happening when one real AI replaces another. The two engines here are ordinary programs. The situations were written by us, not observed in real homes, and how much weight the risky ones carry was our choice. Three of the twelve checks still need a running AI and have not been done. Nothing here proves the wider argument on this page; the judgment stays useful synthesis, not novel.

Who should care

The operating layer becomes the strategic layer.

Models still matter. They become replaceable capabilities inside a household-controlled system of meaning, permissions, actions, evidence, and recovery.

For builders

Build the household operating graph.

Represent people, roles, assets, accounts, context scopes, capabilities, and typed actions. Make permissions and approval criteria executable, then bind replaceable models and devices.

The product is the governed outcome, not the prompt.
For device makers

Expose capability, trust, and action.

Publish what a device can run, which data boundary it satisfies, and which typed actions it supports under actual memory, battery, heat, and foreground-use constraints.

Open interfaces make substitution and continuity real.
For investors

Sovereignty must resist lock-in.

The defensible asset is governed household state, permission relationships, typed actions, outcome evidence, and trusted recovery—only if the household can export it and change suppliers.

A moat built on captivity is not household sovereignty.
